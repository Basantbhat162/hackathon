# Evidence-Backed Problems in Indian Public-Service Digital Journeys (Build What Moves India, Aug 2026)

## Research metadata

- **Research date:** 20 August 2026[^1]
- **Geographic and service coverage:** India; central ministries, state departments, and urban–rural local bodies across identity, health, land, social protection, grievance, municipal, tax, and certification services[^2][^3]
- **Languages searched:** English, Hindi (portals and manuals), plus selective regional content (Marathi, Assamese, Bengali) via state portals and reports[^3][^2]
- **Search rounds completed:** At least 8 focused rounds – national e‑governance overviews; grievance systems (CPGRAMS, sectoral); identity and document services (Aadhaar, DigiLocker, UDID); land and property systems (DILRMP, municipal tax); social protection (PDS, PM‑JAY); voter and civic services (NVSP/VSP); transport (Sarathi/Parivahan); passports and civil registration[^4][^5][^2][^3]
- **Known coverage limits:**
  - No live testing, crawling, or transactions against government systems (hackathon safety rule).
  - Limited visibility into post‑2024 internal fixes unless reflected in official press releases, audits, or updated FAQs.
  - Regional and scheme‑specific issues sampled from available central documents and selected state examples; not a full state‑by‑state census.
  - Prevalence inferred only where a study or audit quantifies it; otherwise marked `ANECDOTAL` or `UNKNOWN` as required.

## Candidate evidence cards

### DR-C01 – Rural e‑governance access failures

- **Problem statement:** Many rural residents cannot reliably complete online public-service journeys (applications, certificates, benefits) due to poor connectivity, inadequate digital literacy, language barriers, and low awareness/trust, forcing dependence on intermediaries and offline workarounds.[^6][^7]
- **Named user and jurisdiction:** A farmer in rural Maharashtra or a senior citizen in rural West Bengal accessing a crop-insurance status page or pension certificate via state or national portals.[^8]
- **Journey start and end:** Start: User attempts to use a web portal or app (e.g., state e‑governance site, UMANG, or CSC-delivered service) from home or a local kiosk to apply or check status. End: User expects a completed transaction (application submitted, certificate downloaded, benefit status checked) without needing multiple physical visits.[^9][^8]
- **Exact failure point:** Connection drops mid-form; portal not mobile-friendly; instructions only in English/Hindi; user cannot interpret required fields or attachments; or portal only reaches an informational stage without enabling transaction completion.[^6][^8]
- **Underlying process, rule, state, responsibility, or backend dependency:**
  - Structural infrastructure gaps: unreliable electricity and internet connectivity in many rural communities.[^6]
  - Lack of sustained digital literacy programmes and multilingual interfaces; many projects digitised existing paper processes without rethinking user-centric flows.[^8][^6]
  - Dependence on CSCs for assisted access, with variation in service quality and additional travel/time costs.[^10][^9]
- **Why this is not only an interface problem:** Core barriers include physical connectivity, socio-linguistic inclusion, and institutional design that digitises bureaucratic procedures without simplifying them, meaning even a perfect UI cannot compensate for lack of power, bandwidth, or literacy.[^8][^6]
- **Documented effect:** Rural communities may be effectively excluded from e‑governance benefits; research highlights that without addressing infrastructure, literacy, language localisation, and trust, online service delivery and participation remain out of reach.[^11][^6]
- **Prevalence status:** `MEASURED` – empirical studies and policy analyses document widespread issues and conceptualise them as systemic rather than isolated.[^6][^8]
- **Evidence supporting the claim:**
  - Empirical study (2022) on rural e‑governance finds poor infrastructure, connectivity, digital literacy, language barriers, and low awareness/trust as key obstacles.[^6]
  - Citizen-centric e‑governance analysis notes that many platforms remain informational, with limited transactional capability; CSCs and BharatNet have expanded access but gaps persist for elderly, women, PwDs, and tribal communities.[^8]
  - Observers highlight digital divide metrics (lower rural internet penetration, device ownership) and socio-economic barriers.[^11]
- **Evidence contradicting or limiting the claim:**
  - Government reports and press releases show large expansion of CSCs and high transaction volumes, indicating significant rural usage for many services.[^9]
  - Penetration of mobile networks and smartphones is growing, potentially narrowing some divides.[^12]
- **Current, historical, resolved, or unclear status:** **Current** – structural challenges remain active in 2025–26, though infrastructure and assisted-access networks are expanding.[^9][^8]
- **Safe prototype boundary and mocked components:**
  - Build an offline-first, multilingual, step-by-step helper that runs locally on device or kiosk and generates structured synthetic applications for common schemes.
  - Mock all backend government services and documents; do not submit anything to live portals.
- **AI role hypothesis:** `USEFUL BUT OPTIONAL` – AI can assist with language translation, voice guidance, and form validation, but the key constraints (connectivity, socio-political design) lie outside AI.
- **Feasibility, correctness risk, confidence, and unanswered questions:**
  - **Feasibility:** High for building a guided kiosk/app that works under poor connectivity; moderate for integrating multiple schemes even in sandboxed form.
  - **Correctness risk:** Medium – danger of misrepresenting government rules or documents if auto-generated guidance is not tightly checked against official sources.
  - **Confidence:** High that digital divide failures are real and persistent, given multiple independent studies.[^8][^6]
  - **Unanswered questions:** Detailed, scheme-by-scheme failure rates in specific districts; effectiveness of recent literacy programmes like PMGDISHA for the most excluded groups.
- **Lived-problem status:** `TEAM CONFIRMATION REQUIRED`.

### DR-C02 – CPGRAMS grievance closure without reasons

- **Problem statement:** CPGRAMS grievances are sometimes closed with directions to approach another office or with no reasons, leaving citizens without a clear explanation or path to effective redress.[^13][^4]
- **Named user and jurisdiction:** Any citizen lodging a grievance on CPGRAMS against a central ministry or participating state department; example: a Maharashtra resident seeking electricity connection who finds multiple grievances marked "Case Closed" without speaking orders.[^14]
- **Journey start and end:** Start: User registers on pgportal.gov.in, files a grievance against a specific authority. End: User expects a reasoned decision explaining actions taken, with clear next steps or appeal rights where needed.[^13]
- **Exact failure point:** Grievance status switches to "Closed" or "Resolved" with either generic advice (e.g., "visit office") or no uploaded Action Taken Report or speaking order; user must refile or appeal without understanding what was done.[^4][^14][^13]
- **Underlying process, rule, state, responsibility, or backend dependency:**
  - Ministries vary widely in grievance-handling frameworks and capacity; some dispose grievances by redirecting citizens to subordinate offices or alternative agencies.[^4]
  - DARPG instructions require valid reasons for closure, but committee reports note non-compliance by many ministries.[^4]
  - CPGRAMS Reform Programme 2022 sets a 30‑day SLA and multi-level appeal structure, yet non-substantive closures remain common failure modes.[^13]
- **Why this is not only an interface problem:** Root issues lie in organisational behaviour, compliance with DARPG instructions, and accountability mechanisms; portal UI alone cannot ensure substantive decision-making or detailed closure notes.[^13][^4]
- **Documented effect:** Citizens have "absolutely no means of knowing" whether grievances were genuinely examined when closures lack reasons, undermining trust and making escalation burdensome; standing committee explicitly flags such behaviour as problematic.[^14][^4]
- **Prevalence status:** `ESTIMATED BY SOURCE` – committee and process guides describe this as common, but precise metrics for closures without reasons are not publicly quantified.[^4][^13]
- **Evidence supporting the claim:**
  - Standing Committee report notes grievances disposed by suggesting approaching another agency, sometimes even sending grievances back to the agency complained against, with many ministries failing to provide reasons for closure.[^4]
  - Step-by-step CPGRAMS guide enumerates "Marked 'Resolved' without action" and "approach office in person" as the most common failure modes.[^13]
  - Public representation details multiple CPGRAMS grievances closed with only "Case Closed" status and no ATR or disposal remarks.[^14]
- **Evidence contradicting or limiting the claim:**
  - DARPG and PIB communications highlight large volumes of grievances resolved within deadlines and ongoing portal reforms, including dashboards and AI-based routing.[^15][^1]
  - CPGRAMS includes appeal levels and encourages feedback, which, if used, can mitigate some closure issues.[^13]
- **Current, historical, resolved, or unclear status:** **Current** – reforms underway, but both committee report and 2026 guidance indicate that non-reasoned closures still occur.[^4][^13]
- **Safe prototype boundary and mocked components:**
  - Build a local tool that helps citizens draft grievances and appeals and then simulate expected closure patterns using synthetic data; do not log in or submit to CPGRAMS.
  - Mock backend grievance states and ATR texts to test user journeys.
- **AI role hypothesis:** `ESSENTIAL` – AI can help classify issues, suggest appropriate ministries/departments, and flag non-substantive closure patterns in synthetic training scenarios.
- **Feasibility, correctness risk, confidence, and unanswered questions:**
  - **Feasibility:** High for a drafting and simulation assistant; integration with live CPGRAMS is explicitly out of scope.
  - **Correctness risk:** Medium – risk of misrouting grievances if recommendation engine does not align with CPGRAMS category structure.
  - **Confidence:** High that closure-quality problems exist, given parliamentary scrutiny and independent process analyses.[^13][^4]
  - **Unanswered questions:** Distribution of closure reasons by ministry; effectiveness of recent DARPG monitoring in improving quality of ATRs.
- **Lived-problem status:** `TEAM CONFIRMATION REQUIRED`.

### DR-C03 – Ayushman Bharat PM‑JAY registration and card issuance problems

- **Problem statement:** Beneficiaries of Ayushman Bharat PM‑JAY experience journey failures where eligibility identification, BIS registration, and e‑card issuance are affected by data errors, duplicate IDs, unrealistic family sizes, and long delays in approving or rejecting applications.[^16][^17][^18]
- **Named user and jurisdiction:** Low-income household in Assam or Bihar seeking Ayushman card via BIS or Common Service Centre.[^19][^16]
- **Journey start and end:** Start: User checks PM‑JAY eligibility (e.g., via NHA portal or CSC), submits registration with Aadhaar and supporting documents. End: User expects timely approval, e‑card issuance, and cashless treatment at empanelled hospitals.[^20][^21]
- **Exact failure point:**
  - BIS beneficiary data contains invalid names, unrealistic dates of birth, duplicate PMJAY IDs, and unrealistic household sizes (11–201 members), indicating poor validation.[^17][^18]
  - No uniform threshold for match confidence score; many approved cases lack scores or have zero; some rejected cases have high scores, causing confusion.[^17]
  - In some states (e.g., Assam), average delays in approving/rejecting registrations reportedly reach around 187 days, with rejection processing delays up to 404 days.[^22][^19]
- **Underlying process, rule, state, responsibility, or backend dependency:**
  - Beneficiary base initially tied to SECC 2011 data, which may be outdated by 2018–21 and misaligned with current poverty/eligibility.[^17]
  - Match confidence scoring and BIS validation controls not consistently implemented, leading to over/under‑inclusion.[^17]
  - State Health Agencies (SHAs) face personnel shortfalls, affecting timely handling of registrations and rejections.[^19]
- **Why this is not only an interface problem:** Core challenges involve data governance, validation algorithms, inter-agency coordination, and staffing; front-end design cannot by itself fix duplicate IDs or delayed approvals.
- **Documented effect:**
  - CAG and analyses note that ineligible households were registered and availed benefits ranging from ₹0.12 lakh to ₹22.44 crore, while eligible households faced delays or rejections.[^18][^17]
  - Beneficiary grievances show that eligibility and registration issues, often tied to card issuance problems, formed a major share of complaints during certain years.[^16][^19]
- **Prevalence status:** `MEASURED` – audits and grievance studies quantify errors and delays at national and state levels.[^18][^16][^17]
- **Evidence supporting the claim:**
  - Performance audit summary points to widespread errors, duplicate IDs, unrealistic family sizes, and registration of ineligible households.[^18]
  - Grievance analysis of 1.1 lakh PM‑JAY grievances identifies eligibility/registration and card-issuance issues as dominant in some states and years.[^16][^19]
  - Secondary articles synthesise CAG findings on match-confidence inconsistencies and delayed approvals.[^22][^17]
- **Evidence contradicting or limiting the claim:**
  - NHA press release refutes some interpretations (e.g., mobile-number-based identification) and emphasises Aadhaar-based e‑KYC and multiple authentication options.[^23]
  - Scheme expansion decisions (e.g., NFSA-based extension to 12 crore families) show ongoing efforts to widen coverage.[^24]
- **Current, historical, resolved, or unclear status:** **Current but evolving** – audit period covers 2018–21; subsequent clarifications and reforms aim to address validation and data-quality concerns.[^25][^23]
- **Safe prototype boundary and mocked components:**
  - Build a synthetic BIS-like registry with controlled data errors to explore better validation rules and user-facing explanations of registration status.
  - Mock hospital and claim systems; no calls to NHA or state PM‑JAY platforms.
- **AI role hypothesis:** `USEFUL BUT OPTIONAL` – AI could help detect anomalies (e.g., unrealistic family sizes), but must be constrained by transparent, rule-based validation.
- **Feasibility, correctness risk, confidence, and unanswered questions:**
  - **Feasibility:** High for creating synthetic registries and dashboards; moderate for replicating complex state-specific workflows.
  - **Correctness risk:** High if AI or rules misclassify real-world eligibility or inadvertently encode bias; prototypes must stay decoupled from actual beneficiary decisions.
  - **Confidence:** High that data-validation and delay issues affected many journeys during the audit period, based on multiple sources.[^16][^18][^17]
  - **Unanswered questions:** Extent of remediation after CAG report; current average approval/rejection times per state.
- **Lived-problem status:** `TEAM CONFIRMATION REQUIRED`.

### DR-C04 – Land-record digitisation and mutation journey failures

- **Problem statement:** Landowners and buyers attempting online land-record and mutation journeys encounter fragmented data, slow digitisation of mutations and maps, and mismatches between textual records and on-ground reality.[^26]
- **Named user and jurisdiction:** Small landholder or urban buyer in a state implementing DILRMP, using online land-record portals to verify ownership and mutations.[^27][^26]
- **Journey start and end:** Start: User attempts to view land records, confirm ownership, and initiate/verify mutation digitally (e.g., via state land-record portal). End: User expects an up-to-date, accurate online record reflecting recent transactions and linked maps.[^26]
- **Exact failure point:**
  - While 86% of land records had been computerised, only 47% of mutation records, 28% of digitally signed Records of Rights (RoR), and 26% of map–RoR linkages had been completed; real-time updation of RoR and maps stood at 15%.[^26]
  - Discrepancies between textual records and spatial maps due to outdated surveys and incomplete updates.[^27][^26]
  - Citizens must navigate multiple departments—Registration, Revenue, Survey—whose records may not be synchronised.[^26]
- **Underlying process, rule, state, responsibility, or backend dependency:**
  - Presumptive titling; land-transaction registration focuses on sale deeds rather than guaranteed titles, with onus on buyers to verify ownership history.[^26]
  - Land data dispersed across Revenue, Registration, and Survey departments working in silos; updates in one department may not propagate to others.[^26]
  - Slow progress and uneven capacity under DILRMP; data inconsistencies and technical infrastructure limitations persist.[^27]
- **Why this is not only an interface problem:** The key issues involve legal titling regimes, fragmented institutional responsibilities, and incomplete digitisation; UI improvements cannot resolve missing or conflicting backend records.
- **Documented effect:**
  - Land disputes constitute a large portion of court cases; unclear records cause delays in infrastructure projects and complicate access to credit and insurance.[^26]
  - Obtaining land-ownership certificates can take months in some cities, indicating long-running process delays.[^26]
- **Prevalence status:** `MEASURED` – official data quantifies digitisation completion percentages; policy analyses link these to widespread issues.[^27][^26]
- **Evidence supporting the claim:**
  - PRS report summarising DILRMP status and underlying legal/admin issues.[^26]
  - Analysis noting 94% digitised land records but persistent data inconsistencies and technical-capacity barriers, especially in some regions.[^27]
- **Evidence contradicting or limiting the claim:**
  - Digitisation progress since 2017 has improved coverage; some states achieve higher integration levels than national averages.[^27]
- **Current, historical, resolved, or unclear status:** **Current but improving** – structural titling and integration challenges remain even as digitisation expands.[^27][^26]
- **Safe prototype boundary and mocked components:**
  - Create a sandbox land-record system with synthetic parcels, textual and spatial data, and controlled inconsistencies; explore user-facing warnings and workflow redesign.
  - Do not attempt any live registry updates or real parcel lookup.
- **AI role hypothesis:** `USEFUL BUT OPTIONAL` – AI can help flag probable inconsistencies and suggest checking additional records; legal and institutional reforms remain primary.
- **Feasibility, correctness risk, confidence, and unanswered questions:**
  - **Feasibility:** High for simulation; low for comprehensive representation of all states.
  - **Correctness risk:** Medium – misrepresenting legal standing of ownership if tool is misused; must be clearly labelled as advisory/synthetic.
  - **Confidence:** High that fragmentation and incomplete digitisation cause real journey failures.[^27][^26]
  - **Unanswered questions:** Up-to-date state-wise metrics beyond 2017; effectiveness of recent integration pilots.
- **Lived-problem status:** `TEAM CONFIRMATION REQUIRED`.

### DR-C05 – Birth certificate digitisation and tracking gaps

- **Problem statement:** Residents needing birth certificates for passports, visas, or education often face bottlenecks when older records are not digitised, verification is slow, and online tracking is limited; only recently have some municipalities introduced more transparent digital tracking systems.[^28][^29][^30]
- **Named user and jurisdiction:** Mumbai resident seeking a corrected, QR-enabled birth certificate; or resident in another city using CRS/municipal portals.[^29][^30]
- **Journey start and end:** Start: User searches for existing birth record, submits an application or correction request online or at CFC, and seeks a digitally signed certificate. End: User expects to download an updated certificate or collect it without repeated office visits.[^30][^28][^29]
- **Exact failure point:**
  - Older records (pre‑2016) require additional verification and may not be immediately available in digital systems, causing delays.[^29][^30]
  - Historically, applicants had to visit medical officers’ offices repeatedly for updates; manual processes made pending applications hard to monitor.[^30]
  - CRS availability varies by city; online registration and tracking may not be uniformly active.[^28][^29]
- **Underlying process, rule, state, responsibility, or backend dependency:**
  - Birth and death registration handled by local bodies with delegated registrars; digitisation and portal design differ widely by municipality.[^29]
  - Legacy records must be digitised and verified before issuance of QR-enabled certificates, requiring manual intervention.[^30]
- **Why this is not only an interface problem:** Backend dependency on legacy logbooks, capacity of registrars, and verification workflows drive the delays; front-end UI alone cannot accelerate them.
- **Documented effect:** Press reporting describes wedding/visa plans being delayed due to difficulty obtaining timely birth certificates, prompting BMC to introduce online tracking via UANs and CFC-based workflows.[^30]
- **Prevalence status:** `ANECDOTAL` – clearly documented in Mumbai; broader national prevalence not quantified.
- **Evidence supporting the claim:**
  - News report highlights citizen inconvenience and BMC’s response, adding tracking and SMS alerts at CFCs; older CRS tasks still take longer due to verification.[^30]
  - Explanatory article notes that non-digitised birth records are not available online until verified and ported by local officials.[^29]
- **Evidence contradicting or limiting the claim:**
  - CRS national portal supports online registration and certificate issuance in many areas, and some municipalities offer full online application and download.[^28]
- **Current, historical, resolved, or unclear status:** **Transitional** – partially historical (manual bottlenecks) but still current for legacy records; new systems aim to reduce delays.[^30]
- **Safe prototype boundary and mocked components:**
  - Build a simulator for CRS/municipal applications and their statuses using synthetic data, to test clearer communication of verification stages and expected timelines.
  - Mock certificate formats; no interaction with real CRS databases.
- **AI role hypothesis:** `USEFUL BUT OPTIONAL` – AI can help predict timelines based on application type and record age, and generate clear notifications, but legal issuance remains human.
- **Feasibility, correctness risk, confidence, and unanswered questions:**
  - **Feasibility:** High for designing a clear status-tracking layer; limited need for complex backend.
  - **Correctness risk:** Low, if clearly labelled as simulation; moderate if users misinterpret synthetic timing predictions as commitments.
  - **Confidence:** Medium – strong evidence for one major city; extrapolation to others is uncertain.
  - **Unanswered questions:** Actual processing times and backlog sizes across varied municipalities.
- **Lived-problem status:** `TEAM CONFIRMATION REQUIRED`.

### DR-C06 – Passport grievance and status-journey failures

- **Problem statement:** Passport applicants experience confusion and delays when police verification, capacity constraints, and multi-channel grievance systems result in long processing times without clear, actionable reasons.[^31][^32][^33]
- **Named user and jurisdiction:** Applicant for a normal passport with pre-police verification, using Passport Seva, PSK, call centre, and grievance portals.[^33][^31]
- **Journey start and end:** Start: User submits application online, books PSK appointment, completes biometrics, and awaits issuance. End: User expects passport issuance within indicative timelines, or reasoned grievance responses if delayed.[^32][^31]
- **Exact failure point:**
  - Delays in receiving police verification reports within 21 days, incomplete police reports due to applicants’ incomplete information, increasing demand, and manpower shortages at passport offices.[^32]
  - Grievances may be handled via Passport Seva portal, CPGRAMS, call centre, helpdesk, and email, leading to bouncing across channels if not carefully routed.[^34][^31][^33]
- **Underlying process, rule, state, responsibility, or backend dependency:**
  - Pre-police verification is mandatory for certain passport categories; passport issuance time depends on timely police reports.[^32]
  - Grievance redress is shared among Passport Seva’s online grievance system, CPGRAMS, call centres, and dedicated grievance cells.[^31][^33][^32]
- **Why this is not only an interface problem:** Root causes lie in police workflows, staffing, and cross-channel coordination; UI improvements cannot directly accelerate verification or unify responses without institutional change.
- **Documented effect:** Government acknowledged "inordinate delay" in some cases, traced to police-report delays, incomplete documentation, high demand, and manpower shortages; strengthened grievance systems and call centres were introduced as remedies.[^32]
- **Prevalence status:** `HISTORICAL/ONGOING` – official Q&A refers to pre-2013 issues; current data suggests improved timelines but residual problems likely.
- **Evidence supporting the claim:**
  - Lok Sabha Q&A details systemic reasons for passport delays and outlines grievance mechanisms.[^32]
  - Official grievance and call-centre documentation underscores multi-channel architecture and escalations.[^33][^31]
- **Evidence contradicting or limiting the claim:**
  - PSP reforms and Passport Seva rollout reportedly improved average issuance times and grievance handling; current performance metrics may show better outcomes.
- **Current, historical, resolved, or unclear status:** **Unclear** – root structural factors persist, but magnitude of delay has likely reduced; real-time metrics not fully public.
- **Safe prototype boundary and mocked components:**
  - Create a synthetic passport journey simulator with possible states (under police verification, pending, issued) and grievance routing advice.
  - No live status checks or real ARN use.
- **AI role hypothesis:** `USEFUL BUT OPTIONAL` – AI can help choose appropriate grievance channels and summarise official Q&A, but cannot change verification timelines.
- **Feasibility, correctness risk, confidence, and unanswered questions:**
  - **Feasibility:** High for building guidance tools; low for guaranteeing outcomes.
  - **Correctness risk:** Medium – misguidance on expected timelines or correct grievance forum.
  - **Confidence:** Medium – strong historical evidence; up-to-date impact is uncertain.
  - **Unanswered questions:** Current average processing times and share of cases breaching SLAs.
- **Lived-problem status:** `TEAM CONFIRMATION REQUIRED`.

### DR-C07 – e‑PDS/AePDS ration journey fragility

- **Problem statement:** Digitised PDS journeys using e‑PoS and Aadhaar authentication can fail if devices malfunction, connectivity is poor, or card/beneficiary data are not correctly synchronised, affecting foodgrain access.[^35][^36][^37]
- **Named user and jurisdiction:** NFSA beneficiary in a rural or urban FPS using AePDS/e‑PoS.[^36][^35]
- **Journey start and end:** Start: Beneficiary visits FPS, authenticates via Aadhaar/e‑PoS, and expects allocated foodgrains. End: Transaction recorded digitally, entitlement received.[^35]
- **Exact failure point:** Authentication fails due to biometric mismatch or device problems; connectivity prevents transaction recording; card or household details in digitised systems do not match entitlement records.
- **Underlying process, rule, state, responsibility, or backend dependency:**
  - Nearly all ration cards are digitised and FPSs automated with e‑PoS; distribution relies on real-time Aadhaar authentication and central database updates.[^35]
  - State portals expose ration card search and grievance utilities, implying complex backend logic.[^36]
- **Why this is not only an interface problem:** Hardware reliability, biometric inclusion, and network availability drive failure risks; UI cannot resolve unavailability of electricity or mismatched biometrics.
- **Documented effect:** Official data emphasise near-universal automation and Aadhaar-authenticated distribution, but do not quantify failure rates; independent accounts suggest potential exclusion where biometric or device issues arise.[^38][^35]
- **Prevalence status:** `UNKNOWN` – adoption is high; precise exclusion metrics via device or biometric failures are not given.
- **Evidence supporting the claim:**
  - PIB outlines extent of e‑PoS adoption, implying heavy reliance on devices and authentication.[^35]
  - State portals and analyses mention data-inconsistency and infrastructure challenges under DILRMP-like programmes.[^36][^27]
- **Evidence contradicting or limiting the claim:**
  - Government reports highlight successful automation and high share of Aadhaar-authenticated transactions.[^35]
- **Current, historical, resolved, or unclear status:** **Current** – automation active; resilience under adverse conditions still a concern.[^35]
- **Safe prototype boundary and mocked components:**
  - Simulate ration-card and FPS interactions in synthetic data, focusing on fallback mechanisms (e.g., offline logs, alternate IDs) without hitting live PDS systems.
- **AI role hypothesis:** `UNPROVEN` – reliability improvements may be more about hardware and process; AI could help in anomaly detection.
- **Feasibility, correctness risk, confidence, and unanswered questions:**
  - **Feasibility:** Medium – needs careful modelling of entitlements and fallback rules.
  - **Correctness risk:** High if prototypes misrepresent NFSA rules or entitlements.
  - **Confidence:** Medium – infrastructure reliance is clear; concrete failure rates remain undocumented.
  - **Unanswered questions:** Real-world statistics on failed authentication and the handling of such cases.
- **Lived-problem status:** `TEAM CONFIRMATION REQUIRED`.
