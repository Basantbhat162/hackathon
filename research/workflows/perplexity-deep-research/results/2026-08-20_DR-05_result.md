# Evidence Audit of Nine Candidate Problems for "Build What Moves India" Hackathon (Status as of 20 August 2026)[^1]

## Overview

This report cross-validates nine candidate public-service problems against current official evidence as of 20 August 2026. It focuses on whether each candidate describes one concrete, current journey with a proven failure in process, rule, handoff, or guidance that can safely be addressed by a mock-data prototype in a hackathon setting. The audit applies the user’s Tier A–E evidence framework and marks candidates as KEEP, NARROW, or DROP based on verification quality and fit with the hackathon boundary.[^2][^3][^4]

## Survivor Evidence Cards

### S2-C02: UDID Medical-Assessment Queue Opacity

#### Named user and jurisdiction

- User: Adult person with disability (PwD) applying for a fresh UDID card and disability certificate.
- Jurisdiction: UDID portal under Department of Empowerment of Persons with Disabilities (DEPwD), Government of India; hospital/CMO in a specific district (e.g., Delhi or Karnataka).[^21][^22]

#### Journey start and end

- Start: User has submitted a UDID application online, sees status as "Submitted" or similar, and is waiting for medical assessment scheduling.[^21]
- End: User has clarity on current process stage, knows preparation requirements, and understands what follow-up is officially allowed (visit to hospital/CMO, status checks, corrections) without assuming any fixed timeline.[^20]

#### Current official steps and timeline

Official UDID FAQs describe the sequence:[^20]

1. User submits application through UDID portal for fresh card or renewal; application goes to notified medical authority/CMO/DMO or designated hospital.[^20]
2. Medical authority verifies application, assigns specialist doctor(s) for assessment, and schedules an assessment date.[^20]
3. Specialist doctor assesses the applicant; report goes to the medical board, which decides disability type, percentage, and certificate validity.[^20]
4. Based on assessment, CMO/DMO fills details; UDID card and certificate are generated electronically and dispatched by DEPwD via Speed Post.[^20]

The same FAQ explicitly states that "as on date, there is no fix timeline" for receiving the medical-assessment call from the hospital. DEPwD’s 2026 description of UDID confirms that medical boards use the portal to process applications and issue cards, but does not introduce any strict time-to-call SLA.[^21][^20]

#### Exact failure point and proven cause

Failure point:

- User-facing opacity between "Submitted" status and actual scheduling or communication of assessment date.[^20]

Proven cause (Tier A):

- System design: official FAQ confirms that there is no fixed timeline for medical-assessment calls, meaning the portal itself does not promise predictable scheduling.[^20]
- Process split: responsibility shifts from portal to local medical authority (CMO/DMO or hospital), which may have its own capacity constraints and batch scheduling practices not visible to the user.[^21][^20]

Additional supporting cause (Tier C):

- Institutional and explainer guides note that applicants must proactively visit the chosen hospital/CMO when status shows "Submitted" to get verification and assessment scheduled, highlighting that online submission alone rarely completes the process.[^23][^24]

This is proven design opacity rather than a formally documented failure; the portal admits lack of timeline but does not describe how queue progression is communicated.[^20]

#### Scale status

- Official evidence documents lack of fixed timeline but do not quantify delays or queue length.[^20]
- Explainer articles and practice-oriented guides repeatedly mention waiting and follow-up needs but without representative statistics.[^24][^23]

Scale classification: **ESTIMATED BY SOURCE** (queue opacity and delay experience are widely described by secondary sources with method-based walkthroughs but not yet quantified in Tier A statistics).

#### Evidence supporting and limiting the claim

Supporting evidence:

- UDID FAQ from swavlambancard.gov.in explicitly lists the stages of application, verification, assessment, and card generation, and confirms no fixed timeline for medical assessment calls.[^20]
- DEPwD’s official description of UDID emphasises hospital and medical-board workflow but does not offer timeframe commitments, reinforcing that schedule visibility is decentralised.[^21]
- Multiple institutional explainer guides (e.g., NEET disability card guidance, general UDID card application explainers) instruct applicants to monitor status and then visit hospital/CMO for verification and assessment scheduling, implying that proactive follow-up is part of the de facto process.[^22][^23]

Limiting evidence:

- No official SLA or statistical report on median waiting time from "Submitted" to assessment.[^20]
- No published queue metrics or dashboards for UDID, unlike some other schemes with public progress reports.[^21]
- First-person accounts exist but are Tier D and may not generalise.[^24]

#### Government changes and existing alternatives

Government changes:

- UDID portal and national database initiative have standardised application, assessment, and card generation, replacing fragmented local disability certificates.[^21]
- The portal supports status tracking and locating medical authorities, which is an improvement over purely paper-based systems.[^55][^21]

Existing alternatives:

- Official portal status views and FAQs.[^55][^20]
- Explainer guides from educational and disability-support institutions showing step-by-step application and hospital follow-up.[^22][^23]

These alternatives do not model queue scenarios or provide synthetic case-based guidance on what a user at a given status can safely do next.

#### Remaining gap

- Lack of an accessible, case-based explainer that translates portal status into concrete, permissible actions (e.g., "Your status is Submitted for 45 days: you may visit hospital X with these documents and ask about assessment scheduling; do not expect any legally guaranteed date").[^22][^20]
- No synthetic queue visualisation that shows variations in pathways (e.g., correction required, reassessment needed, card dispatch) without promising timelines.[^20]

The hackathon prototype would explicitly encode "no fixed timeline" as a design constraint and focus on stage-aware preparation and follow-up, not on forecasting waits.

#### Safe prototype boundary

- One named synthetic user (e.g., "Ravi, 28, locomotor disability") in one district with a selected hospital.
- Journey from portal submission to card dispatch using fully synthetic application IDs, hospital codes, assessment events, and dates.
- No use of real Aadhaar, medical records, or UDID credentials; no live calls to UDID APIs.
- Prototype runs locally or on test data; users in production would manually transpose advice onto their context.

#### Functional and mocked components

Functional (logic-only, no live integration):

- Status interpreter: maps synthetic statuses like "Submitted", "Under Verification", "Assessment Scheduled", "Card Generated" to official steps and allowed follow-up actions.[^20]
- Guidance generator: rule-based messages explaining what documents to carry, whom to approach (CMO/DMO, hospital section), and what questions to ask, derived strictly from FAQs.[^21][^20]
- Accessibility: text-size controls and language toggles; offline-first deployment to handle slow networks.

Mocked:

- Queue visualisation: timeline or stages showing movement without actual wait times.
- Synthetic notifications: mock SMS/WhatsApp-style updates triggered by stage changes.
- Synthetic portal screens: fake UDID dashboard UI reproducing public layouts without real data.

#### AI necessity explanation

- AI is **helpful**, not essential.
- Core mapping from status to actions can be deterministic, based on official FAQs and DEPwD descriptions.[^21][^20]
- Limited runtime AI could assist in:
  - Translating explanations into local languages.
  - Summarising complex rules into accessible text for PwDs with cognitive or language barriers.

Given correctness risk, deterministic rules should dominate; AI must not infer timelines or medical decisions.

#### Measurable before-and-after comparison

- Baseline metric (mock or pilot evaluation, not national statistics): proportion of users who can correctly answer:
  - Their current process stage.
  - Which authority they must approach next.
  - Whether a fixed timeline applies.

- After using prototype, measure improvement in correct answers via controlled user testing.
- Track reduction in inappropriate actions (e.g., filing RTI before visiting hospital, assuming guaranteed assessment dates).

#### Under-60-second demo moment

- Show synthetic UDID dashboard where status is "Submitted" for 30 days.
- In under 60 seconds, prototype:
  - Identifies stage.
  - Explains that no fixed timeline exists.
  - Lists safe next step: visit selected hospital’s disability-certification section with specific documents.

#### Correctness guardrails

- Hard-coded statement from UDID FAQ: "As on date, there is no fix timeline for getting medical assessment call" displayed in every guidance view.[^20]
- All advice tagged with source references (visible in UI) to prevent overreach beyond FAQs and DEPwD site.[^21][^20]
- No prediction of dates, probability of approval, or disability percentage.
- Clear disclaimer that decisions rest with medical authorities and that prototype is a learning tool, not an official system.

#### Team lived-problem question

- Team must confirm: "Has any team member or close associate personally gone through UDID application and medical-assessment scheduling in the chosen district since 2023?" If not, mark TE
AM CONFIRMATION REQUIRED and seek co-design input from local disability organisations.

#### Confidence and remaining unknowns

Confidence:

- High that the journey stages and lack of timeline are correctly represented, based on Tier A FAQs and DEPwD descriptions.[^21][^20]

Unknowns:

- Actual distribution of wait times by district and disability category.
- Local variations in hospital practices (e.g., walk-in scheduling vs. phone calls).
- Specific accessibility needs of PwDs in different contexts.

Prototype should highlight these unknowns and avoid encoding assumptions.

### S2-C05: e-Shram Registration Barriers

#### Named user and jurisdiction

- User: Unorganised worker (e.g., construction helper) attempting to register or update profile on e-Shram.
- Jurisdiction: e-Shram portal under Ministry of Labour & Employment; CSC/SSK network for assisted registration; national scope, but prototype can constrain to one state for address fields.[^35][^36]

#### Journey start and end

- Start: Worker attempts self-registration or profile update and faces barriers related to Aadhaar-linked mobile, bank details, occupation selection, language comprehension, or lack of digital familiarity.[^36][^35]
- End: Worker or assisted operator (CSC VLE) successfully completes registration or update using appropriate self-service or assisted paths, with clarity on which route applies given their constraints.[^35]

#### Current official steps and timeline

Official FAQs and registration portal specify:[^36][^35]

1. Worker visits e-Shram portal or CSC/SSK.
2. Essential documents: Aadhaar number, Aadhaar-linked mobile number (for OTP), and bank account details.[^35][^36]
3. Self-registration: enter Aadhaar-linked mobile, validate OTP, provide personal details, occupation, address, and bank information, and receive UAN.[^36][^35]
4. Assisted registration: if Aadhaar-linked mobile is absent, worker can visit CSC/SSK and register using biometric authentication.[^35][^36]
5. Post-registration updates: workers can log in with UAN and OTP or via Aadhaar-based update to change mobile, address, bank, occupation, and other details.[^18][^36]

No specific statutory timeline for registration is stated; process is designed to be immediate upon successful OTP/biometric authentication.[^35]

#### Exact failure point and proven cause

Failure points:

- No Aadhaar-linked mobile for OTP; worker does not know assisted route.[^56][^35]
- Incorrect or inactive bank account details; profile update path unclear.[^18]
- Confusion about occupation category and activity selection impacting scheme linkages.[^18]
- Language barriers and digital inexperience causing navigation errors.[^35]

Proven causes (Tier A/B mix):

- Aadhaar-linked mobile requirement documented in FAQs; absence triggers need for CSC biometric registration.[^36][^35]
- Bank and occupation update options documented in FAQs and portal, but workers may not know how to use them.[^18][^36]

Recurrence evidence (Tier C):

- Civil guides and CSC-focused materials show repeated emphasis on Aadhaar-linked mobile, biometric fallback, and CSC role in bridging gaps.[^57][^58]

#### Scale status

- Government does not publish detailed statistics on failed e-Shram registrations due to mobile/bank/occupation barriers.
- However, explicit provision of CSC/SSK assisted registration and dedicated helpdesk suggests recognised scale.[^18][^35]

Scale classification: **UNKNOWN** (barriers are structurally acknowledged but not quantitatively measured in public Tier A documents).

#### Evidence supporting and limiting the claim

Supporting evidence:

- FAQs explicitly require Aadhaar-linked mobile or CSC biometric registration, confirming that lack of linked mobile is a recognised barrier with a formal workaround.[^36][^35]
- Official documents describe profile-update features for mobile, bank, occupation, and address.[^18][^36]
- CSC guidance emphasises use of Aadhaar-linked mobile and biometric authentication for artisans and workers.[^57]

Limiting evidence:

- No official breakdown of error rates or proportion of workers needing CSC-assisted registration vs self-registration.[^35]
- Barriers like language or digital literacy are inferred from design choices (assisted paths and helpdesks) rather than measured.[^18]

#### Government changes and existing alternatives

Government changes:

- Launch of e-Shram with integrated Aadhaar-based registration and CSC-assisted paths to accommodate workers lacking digital access.[^35]
- Establishment of national helpdesk and grievance portal for e-Shram issues.[^18]

Existing alternatives:

- Official self-registration portal and assisted CSC network.[^18][^35]
- Numerous tutorials and commercial blogs explaining registration steps and documents.[^25][^58]

These alternatives provide general guidance but not personalised diagnostic flows based on specific barrier combinations.

#### Remaining gap

- Lack of a barrier-aware diagnostic tool that can take a synthetic profile (e.g., no Aadhaar-linked mobile, uncertain occupation, unverified bank), match it to official rules, and output the proper combination of self-service, CSC visit, and helpdesk/grievance steps.[^35][^18]
- No user-facing simulation that lets VLEs and workers rehearse registration with mock data, reducing errors when handling real Aadhaar and bank details.

#### Safe prototype boundary

- One synthetic worker per session, with configurable attributes: has Aadhaar, has Aadhaar-linked mobile, has bank account, has preferred occupation descriptor, language preference.
- Logic restricted to publicly documented rules: self-registration vs CSC biometric, helpdesk, grievance portal.[^18][^35]
- No live OTP or Aadhaar/UIDAI calls; all authentication simulated.

#### Functional and mocked components

Functional:

- Rule engine mapping synthetic profile to official path: e.g., "No Aadhaar-linked mobile → CSC biometric registration".[^35]
- Interface for selecting occupation and mapping to standard categories, referencing official occupation guidance.[^18]
- Explanation screens referencing FAQs for each barrier and fix.

Mocked:

- Synthetic e-Shram registration screens and UAN generation.
- Synthetic helpdesk interactions and grievance ticket examples.

#### AI necessity explanation

- AI classified as **helpful**.
- Deterministic rules can cover primary barriers; AI can assist in:
  - Natural-language explanation of rules in local languages.
  - Dialogue-based troubleshooting for multi-barrier scenarios.

To minimise risk, AI should be constrained to explanation, not rule generation.

#### Measurable before-and-after comparison

- User-testing metric: proportion of workers/VLEs who can correctly identify their path (self-service vs CSC) and required documents before and after using the prototype.
- Reduced incidence of synthetic errors (e.g., starting self-registration without Aadhaar-linked mobile) in training sessions.

#### Under-60-second demo moment

- Demo synthetic worker who has Aadhaar but no Aadhaar-linked mobile and uncertain bank details.
- Prototype quickly routes them to CSC biometric registration, lists required documents, and shows mock confirmation of successful registration.

#### Correctness guardrails

- All decisions tagged with citations to FAQs and official documents visible in UI.[^35][^18]
- Explicit warning that actual registration must occur on official e-Shram portal or CSC systems; prototype is for learning only.
- No inference of eligibility for schemes beyond what e-Shram itself indicates.

#### Team lived-problem question

- Team must confirm: "Has any member or close associate navigated e-Shram registration or updates (including CSC-assisted registration) since 2023?" If not, mark TEAM CONFIRMATION REQUIRED and seek field input from worker organisations.

#### Confidence and remaining unknowns

Confidence:

- High that core rules about Aadhaar-linked mobile, CSC biometric fallback, and update paths are captured from Tier A sources.[^18][^35]

Unknowns:

- True error rates and distribution of barriers in different regions.
- How CSC practices vary across districts.

Prototype should emphasise that it encodes national rules and typical practices, not local exceptions.

### S2-C08: NSP Authentication and Status Diagnostics

#### Named user and jurisdiction

- User: Student in India applying for a scholarship through NSP from AY 2024–25 onward.
- Jurisdiction: National Scholarship Portal (NSP) under Ministry of Education and MeitY; OTR via NSP OTR app and AadhaarFaceRD; PFMS for payment.[^49][^50]

#### Journey start and end

- Start: Student attempts to generate OTR via face authentication, encounters errors (e.g., error 904, biometric lock), then faces login and status-interpretation issues for scholarship application.[^50][^59]
- End: Student understands official OTR requirements, resolves basic face-authentication and login problems, and can interpret application status and payment issues using official guidance.[^60][^49]

#### Current official steps and timeline

Official OTR guidelines and portal announcements describe:[^61][^49][^50]

1. OTR is mandatory for all NSP services and serves as a unique 14-digit identifier for the student’s academic career.[^49][^61]
2. OTR generation requires an active mobile number, Aadhaar or Aadhaar enrolment ID, and completion of OTP-based eKYC followed by face authentication through NSP OTR app and AadhaarFaceRD.[^50][^49]
3. Students receive a reference number after OTP eKYC, then use NSP OTR app and AadhaarFaceRD for face authentication; on success, OTR is generated and sent by SMS.[^61][^50]
4. Portal announcements identify error 904 and attribute many face-auth failures to Aadhaar biometric lock; students must unlock biometrics via mAadhaar or My Aadhaar before retrying.[^59][^50]
5. NSP helpdesk and FAQs describe login processes, status codes (Pending at Institute/State/Ministry, Approved, Rejected, Under Verification), and payment tracking via PFMS.[^62][^60]

No explicit temporal SLA for OTR generation beyond technical completion of steps; scholarship application timelines are scheme-specific.[^49]

#### Exact failure point and proven cause

Failure points:

- Face-authentication errors such as error 904 and general failure due to biometric lock, outdated AadhaarFaceRD app, or poor camera/lighting conditions.[^59][^50]
- Confusion about OTR vs application ID and status codes.[^60]

Proven causes (Tier A):

- Official NSP announcements explicitly link error 904 to outdated AadhaarFaceRD app and biometric lock, instructing students to update the app and unlock biometrics before retry.[^50][^59]
- OTR guidelines emphasise that OTR is mandatory and must be generated via Aadhaar-based eKYC and face authentication.[^61][^49]

Recurrence evidence (Tier C):

## Corrections to Discovery Stage Claims

1. **CPGRAMS resolution timeline:**
   - Discovery claim: 30-day SLA for CPGRAMS resolution.
   - Correction: DARPG’s Comprehensive Guidelines (August 2024) reduced SLA from 30 days to 21 days; CPGRAMS FAQ now shows 21 days as prescribed limit, with interim replies required when delays occur.[^9][^68][^69]

2. **CPGRAMS closure-quality problem:**
   - Discovery inference: widespread non-substantive closures under legacy CPGRAMS.
   - Correction: NextGen CPGRAMS reforms introduced feedback call centre, appeal mechanisms, and quality-focused metrics; while anecdotal non-substantive closures may remain, Tier A evidence emphasises improved average resolution times (13–15 days) and citizen satisfaction indices rather than persistent systemic closure failure.[^70][^71][^72]

3. **UDID wait duration:**
   - Discovery inference: specific average wait times for medical-assessment calls.
   - Correction: UDID FAQs explicitly state there is "no fix timeline" for medical assessment calls; no official measurement of wait duration is published, so any earlier numeric wait claims are unsupported.[^20]

4. **BBMP property-tax refund as evidence of failure frequency:**
   - Discovery inference: existence of refund policy implies frequent reconciliation failures.
   - Correction: BBMP refund and disclaimer pages document workflow for failed and duplicate transactions but do not quantify failure frequency; using policy existence alone as proof of widespread failure is not justified.[^27][^25]

5. **CRS registration-rate contradictions:**
   - Discovery confusion: conflicting claims about low completeness vs near-universal registration.
   - Correction: CRS 2024 report and commentaries show near-universal birth (99.1%) and death (99.4%) registration nationally, while also acknowledging timeliness gaps and under-registration of infant deaths; older low-completeness figures are historical and cannot be used to describe 2026 conditions.[^32][^73][^74]

6. **Parivahan alternate recovery and lockout:**
   - Discovery claim: users fully locked out of mobile-number recovery if old number unavailable.
   - Correction: RC and DL mobile-update facilities allow Aadhaar-based verification and RTO-assisted approval when information is incomplete; official pages explicitly mention online update and RTO involvement, contradicting absolute lockout narratives.[^39][^44][^75]

7. **PDS e-KYC suspension rules:**
   - Discovery claim: ration benefits automatically suspended solely due to e-KYC mismatch.
   - Correction: National e-KYC guidelines describe exception handling and duplicate resolution; civil analysis notes risk of exclusion, but state-specific notifications must be consulted before asserting suspension or appeal rights; evidence from Kerala and other states shows Aadhaar/e-KYC completion as a requirement but emphasises correction windows and field verification.[^47][^54][^76]

8. **NSP errors as evidence of prevalence:**
   - Discovery inference: existence of error codes like 904 implies high prevalence of failures.
   - Correction: NSP announcements acknowledge specific errors and provide fixes but do not quantify prevalence; error existence should be treated as proof of technical possibility, not widespread incidence.[^59][^50]

9. **PF Nikal commercial guidance dominance:**
   - Discovery stage often leaned on commercial fix guides and marketing pages.
   - Correction: PF Nikal must anchor its diagnostic and escalation logic in official EPFO FAQs, EPFiGMS portal, EPFO Citizens’ Charter timelines, and law/RTI frameworks; commercial guides can suggest recurrence but cannot substitute for Tier A sources when defining rejection reasons or grievance steps.[^5][^77][^78][^79][^80]
